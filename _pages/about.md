Hi! I'm a fourth year graduate student at Harvard University working with [Jesse Snedeker](https://sites.harvard.edu/harvardlds/our-labs/snedeker-lab/jesse-snedeker/). 

<img src="{{ base_path }}/images/bluey_eeg.jpg" alt="A photo of me holding a Bluey stuffed animal wearing an EEG cap" style="float: right; width: 38%; margin: 0 0 1em 1.5em;">

My research asks how children understand language -- what goes on in kid's brains when they are hearing someone talking to them, or listening to a story? How do kids understand the words they are hearing? Does this process change from childhood to adulthood? In my area of work, we often call this *sentence processing* -- how do we understand language in real time?

Lots of my research uses electroencephalography (EEG), a neuroimaging method that lets us record and analyze the electrical activity happening in the brain. EEG is awesome for study language, because it's *really fast!* We can actually see what the brain is doing exactly when we hear or see a word. This is important for a lot of the questions I care about, because language is happening really fast!

Before starting graduate school, I was a lab manager at Harvard, and before that I was an undergraduate at the University of Maryland, where I worked with [Kelly Mix](https://education.umd.edu/directory/kelly-mix).
